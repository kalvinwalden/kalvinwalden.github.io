City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson Co., 1989

A Supreme Court case set the rules

Richmond, Virginia required prime contractors on city construction jobs to subcontract at least 30% of the dollars to minority-owned firms. The Supreme Court struck the plan down.

Richmond had compared its population, which was half African American, to the share of prime contracts that went to African American firms. The Court called that the wrong comparison and said where the right one lies.

“Where there is a significant statistical disparity between the number of qualified minority contractors willing and able to perform a particular service and the number of such contractors actually engaged by the locality … an inference of discriminatory exclusion could arise.”

Report Chapter III, pp. 39–42

The right comparison is between qualified firms that are ready, willing and able to do the work, and the dollars those firms actually receive. Any race- or gender-conscious program now needs that evidence to survive strict scrutiny. A disparity study produces it.

Relevant geographic market area

Start with where the City spends

Firms in Dallas aren't Kansas City's competition, so the study first draws the market. Following antitrust practice, the market should capture at least 75% of the City's spending.

I mapped every vendor's ZIP code to its county. 92.84% of prime dollars went to firms in the 11-county Kansas City metro, on both sides of the state line, so that became the market for every industry.

Availability

Count who could do the work

Availability is each group's share of the firms in the market that are ready, willing and able to work for the City.

I built it from a master vendor file: firms the City paid or awarded, its vendor lists, neighboring governments' vendor lists, and six certification directories that record who owns each firm. Every firm is counted once.

Utilization

Count who actually got paid

Utilization is each group's share of the dollars the City actually paid to firms in the market.

Across seven years and five industries that was $5.8 billion in payments, each one traced to a single firm and its ownership group. Construction alone was $1.98 billion.

Disparity index

Divide one by the other

Divide utilization by availability and multiply by 100. At 100, a group is paid in proportion to its presence in the market. Under 100, it is underutilized. Under 80, the report treats the gap as substantial, and every index also goes through a statistical significance test.

African American–owned construction firms landed at 82.9: underutilized, but above the 80 line. The report does not find that gap statistically significant.

Beyond the index

Then test the other explanations

A gap alone isn't proof of discrimination. Smaller firms may lack the capacity for the biggest contracts, so the study reruns the analysis on contracts where size isn't the issue, and later chapters test firm characteristics directly.

Only when a significant gap survives those checks does the City have the factual basis for a race- or gender-conscious remedy.

Calls that moved the results

    

      
One firm, one identity
The same vendor appeared under different spellings across payment, award and certification files.
Without clustering, one firm's dollars split across several IDs and availability is overcounted.

      
People are not firms
Payees who were individuals were tagged with a Naive Bayes name classifier and excluded.
Counting people as firms would inflate the pool of available businesses.

      
Joint ventures, split by share
JV payments were divided among partner firms by ownership percentage, with the original rows tagged for audit.
Crediting a JV's full amount to one partner overstates that group's utilization.

      
Conflicting certifications
When directories disagreed on ownership, the firm was researched and resolved by hand, with race and ethnicity taking priority over gender.
A consistent rule keeps each firm in exactly one group, which the disparity math requires.
